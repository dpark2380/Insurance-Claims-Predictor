Exploratory Data Analysis

French Motor Third-Party Liability Dataset

This notebook explores the French MTPL dataset to understand the data before modelling. The analysis is split into two parts.

  • Part 1 — Severity: Distribution and features of claim amounts (26k claims)
  • Part 2 — Frequency: Which policies result in a claim (678k policies)

Both parts feed into separate models that are later combined to estimate a pure premium.

Frequency Data Quality: Implausible Values & a Join Pitfall

Two corrections are worth making to freMTPL2freq before it’s used for modelling (see e.g. Noll, Salzmann & Wüthrich (2020), and scikit-learn’s own tutorial on this exact dataset):

  1. Implausible ClaimNb/Exposure values. A handful of policies report more than 4 claims or more than 1 year of exposure in a single annual policy. Standard practice for this dataset is to cap rather than drop, since the rest of the record (features) is still usable. (We also assert there are no duplicate IDpol rows in the raw fetch — there aren’t, in this OpenML version — as a defensive check.)
  2. A join pitfall to avoid, not a raw-data defect. Left-joining freMTPL2freq with freMTPL2sev on IDpol fans out one row per claim for any policy with 2+ claims — correct for building the severity dataset (claims_cleaned.csv, one row per claim), but wrong if that same joined table is then saved as the frequency dataset. Doing so silently duplicates ~1,500 policies’ feature rows (once per claim they had), giving them outsized weight in frequency models trained on it. The frequency table (policies_cleaned.csv) should stay one row per policy — so we keep df_freq (deduplicated/capped, pre-join) as the source of truth for frequency modelling and EDA, and only use the joined/fanned-out table for severity.

Claim Amount Distribution

ClaimAmount is heavily right-skewed (mean 1,569 EUR vs median 1,172 EUR), with extreme outliers up to 4M EUR. The top 1% of claims (265 records) were capped at the 99th percentile (16,451 EUR).

The log-transformed distribution is approximately normal, which motivates two modelling choices.

  1. Using a Gamma GLM with log-link rather than ordinary linear regression
  2. Log-transforming the target when training the neural network

Many claims cluster at round amounts (1,204 EUR, 1,128 EUR, 1,172 EUR), likely standardised repair tariffs rather than individually assessed claims. This is a known characteristic of this dataset.

Feature vs Severity: Key Insight

All individual features show near-zero R² against ClaimAmount. This is expected and is a fundamental property of insurance severity modelling — these features predict whether a claim happens (frequency), not how much it costs (severity).

For example, BonusMalus (claims history score) predicts that a risky driver will crash more often, but when they do crash, the repair bill isn’t systematically larger than a careful driver’s.

The signal in severity modelling comes from combining features together, which is what the GLM, XGBoost, and neural network will do in the following notebooks.

Overdispersion Check

The Poisson GLM used in notebook 02 assumes Var(ClaimNb) = Mean(ClaimNb). That assumption is worth checking before relying on it — if the actual variance is substantially larger than the mean, the model is overdispersed, standard errors on the coefficients will be understated, and a Negative Binomial GLM would be the more defensible choice.

from scipy.stats import poisson

mean_claimnb = df_freq['ClaimNb'].mean()
var_claimnb = df_freq['ClaimNb'].var()
dispersion_ratio = var_claimnb / mean_claimnb

print(f'--- Overdispersion Check (ClaimNb, all {len(df_freq):,} policies) ---')
print(f'Mean:             {mean_claimnb:.4f}')
print(f'Variance:         {var_claimnb:.4f}')
print(f'Dispersion ratio (Var/Mean): {dispersion_ratio:.3f}')
print('(Poisson assumes this ratio is 1.0)')

Overdispersion: Findings

Dispersion ratio (Var/Mean) is 1.065, close to the 1.0 that Poisson assumes. This is a large improvement on the 1.33 ratio we saw before fixing the frequency data (removing capped/implausible ClaimNb/Exposure values) — most of the apparent overdispersion was coming from a small number of implausible records (e.g. ClaimNb=16 for a 0.33-year exposure), not from genuine variability in the underlying claim process.

Implication for modelling. With the ratio this close to 1.0, the Poisson GLM’s assumption is now well supported — coefficient standard errors and p-values in notebook 02 can be trusted without needing to reach for a Negative Binomial correction. This is a good example of a “model limitation” that turned out to be a data issue instead.

Claim Frequency Findings

Unlike severity, frequency shows clear and intuitive relationships with individual features.

  • BonusMalus - strongest predictor by far. Drivers with a score of 150+ claim at 19.1% vs 4.5% for clean-record drivers (50–60). This is exactly what the score is designed to measure.
  • DrivAge - young drivers (18–25) claim most often (6.7%), middle-aged drivers (26–35) least (4.2%), with a smaller uptick for 65+ (5.9%). Classic insurance U-shape.
  • Area - a modest gradient from A (4.6%) up to F (5.9%), consistent with denser/higher-traffic areas.
  • VehPower - no clean monotonic trend once the data is corrected; mid-power vehicles (5–10) cluster around 5.3–5.5%, both very low- and very high-power vehicles are slightly lower (~4.1–4.6%).
  • Region - meaningful geographic variation (R53 ~6.1%, R23 ~3.2%), reflecting differences in driving conditions or traffic.
  • VehGas - Regular slightly higher than Diesel (5.3% vs 4.7%).

These features predict whether a claim happens. In the modelling notebooks we build a Poisson GLM to quantify these effects formally, then combine with the severity model to estimate a pure premium.
