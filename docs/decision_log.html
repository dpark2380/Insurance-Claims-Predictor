The one idea the whole project rests on

An insurer needs to know the expected annual cost of covering a policy. That single number is called the pure premium — pure because it excludes expenses, profit and capital loadings. You could try to predict it directly, but almost every policy costs zero (no claim), and the few that don't are wildly variable. That mix of "mostly zeros plus a heavy tail" is hard for one model to fit.

So the problem is split in two. Frequency asks how often a policy claims. Severity asks what a claim costs once it happens. Multiply the two and you have the pure premium. The split isn't just a modelling convenience — the two halves are driven by genuinely different things:

Frequency driven bydriving behaviour
Severity driven byvehicle & repair market
Frequency distributionPoisson (counts)
Severity distributionGamma (positive, skewed)

A driver with a bad claims history crashes more often. But when they do crash, the repair bill isn't systematically bigger than a careful driver's. Notebook 01 shows this empirically, and it turns out to be the single most important finding in the whole project — it explains why every severity model in notebooks 02–04 performs about the same.

The three models, and why three

The same two-stage problem is solved three times, with methods of increasing flexibility and decreasing transparency. The point is not to crown a winner on accuracy; it is to measure what the extra flexibility actually buys, so that the accuracy-versus-explainability trade-off becomes a number rather than an opinion.

The three model families
ModelWhat it can representWhat you give up
GLM Straight-line effects on a log scale; every effect is one coefficient Cannot bend to curves or interactions unless you build them in by hand
XGBoost Curves, thresholds and interactions, found automatically No single coefficient to quote; needs SHAP and partial dependence to explain
Neural net Any smooth function, in principle Effectively a black box; hardest to defend to a regulator

01

Exploratory data analysis

notebooks/01_eda.ipynb → data/claims_cleaned.csv, data/policies_cleaned.csv

This notebook does two jobs: it cleans the raw data into the two tables every other notebook reads, and it checks the assumptions the later models will depend on. Getting the cleaning wrong here quietly poisons everything downstream, so most of the decisions below are about data integrity rather than analysis.

1.1

Keep two separate tables, not one joined table

Theory

The raw data comes in two files: one row per policy (frequency) and one row per claim (severity). Joining them on policy ID fans a policy with two claims out into two rows. That is exactly right for a severity dataset — two claims really are two observations of claim cost. It is wrong for a frequency dataset, where those two rows would let one policy's features vote twice.

Evidence

The join produces 26,444 claim records across only 24,944 distinct policies — about 1,500 policies would have been silently duplicated in the frequency table. The notebook keeps the pre-join, one-row-per-policy table as the frequency source and uses the fanned-out join only for severity.

1.2

Cap implausible claim counts and exposures rather than deleting the rows

Theory

A few records report more than 4 claims, or more than a full year of exposure, on a one-year policy. These are data-entry artefacts, not real risk. But the rest of each record — driver age, vehicle, region — is still perfectly usable, so capping preserves information that deletion would throw away. This is the standard treatment for this dataset in the literature.

Evidence

9 policies capped at 4 claims; 1,224 capped at 1 year of exposure, out of 678,013. A tiny fraction — but decision 1.5 shows it mattered a great deal.

1.3

Cap claim amounts at the 99th percentile

Theory

Claim costs have an extremely heavy right tail, running up to about €4 million. A handful of catastrophic claims would dominate any squared-error loss and make the model chase noise. In practice these are handled separately through reinsurance, not through the everyday rating model, so removing their influence from the pricing model reflects how insurers actually work.

Evidence

The 99th percentile is €16,451.22. Only 265 claims (1.0%) sit above it and are pulled down to that value. Post-cap the distribution has mean €1,569 against median €1,172 — still right-skewed, which is the shape that justifies the Gamma model in notebook 02.

1.4

Drop records where age is 99

Theory

A vehicle age or driver age of exactly 99 is a placeholder for "unknown", not a real value. Unlike the capped fields above there is no sensible substitute, and the count is negligible, so deletion is cleaner than imputation.

Evidence

1 record with vehicle age 99 and 4 with driver age 99, leaving 26,439 clean claims. No missing values anywhere else.

1.5

Test for overdispersion before committing to Poisson

Theory

A Poisson model assumes the variance of the count equals its mean. Real claim counts are often overdispersed — more variable than that — which doesn't bias the coefficients but does understate their standard errors, making effects look more statistically certain than they are. If the ratio were well above 1, a Negative Binomial model would be the defensible choice instead.

Evidence

Mean 0.0532, variance 0.0566, ratio 1.065 — close enough to 1 that Poisson stands. Notably, this ratio was 1.33 before the capping in decision 1.2. Most of the apparent overdispersion was a handful of bad records, not genuine variability. A "model limitation" that turned out to be a data problem.

1.6

Check features against severity and against frequency separately

Theory

Before fitting anything, look at whether the available features relate to each target at all. If a feature has no marginal relationship with the target, no model can conjure one from it — the ceiling on performance is set by the data, not the algorithm.

Evidence

Against severity, every numeric feature fits with an R² of roughly zero. Against frequency, the same features separate risk sharply: claim rate runs 4.5% for the cleanest bonus-malus band up to 19.1% for the worst; young drivers claim at 6.7% against 4.2% for the 26–35 group, with an uptick again at 65+; regions span 3.2% to 6.1%. Overall claim rate is 5.02%.

The finding that shapes the rest of the project

These features predict whether you claim, not how much your claim costs. Bonus-malus is a record of how often you have crashed; it says nothing about the price of the bumper. So expect the frequency models to separate on skill, and the severity models to converge on roughly the same mediocre performance regardless of complexity. Notebooks 02–04 confirm exactly that.

The notebook ends by writing claims_cleaned.csv (26,439 rows, one per claim) and policies_cleaned.csv (678,013 rows, one per policy). Every later notebook starts from these two files and nothing else.

02

GLM — the actuarial baseline

notebooks/02_glm.ipynb → models/glm_*.pkl, results/test_split.npz, results/predictions_glm.npz

A generalised linear model is the industry standard for insurance pricing, and this notebook fits two: a Gamma GLM for severity and a Poisson GLM for frequency. It is the baseline every later model is judged against, and it is also the only model whose logic can be read straight off the coefficient table.

Shared setup

2.1

One-hot encode categoricals with the first level dropped

Theory

A linear model needs numbers, so each category becomes its own 0/1 column. Dropping one level per variable avoids the dummy variable trap: if every level had a column, they would sum to the intercept column and the design matrix would be singular. The dropped level becomes the reference, and every remaining coefficient is read as an effect relative to that reference.

Evidence

9 raw features (5 numeric, 4 categorical) expand to 42 model columns. The same encoding is repeated identically in notebooks 03 and 04 so all three models see the same design matrix.

2.2

Split 80/20 with a fixed random seed of 42

Theory

Test-set metrics are only comparable across models if the models were tested on the same rows. Fixing the seed makes the split reproducible in three separate notebooks running on three separate days, without them having to pass data between each other.

Evidence

Severity: 21,151 train / 5,288 test. Frequency: 542,410 train / 135,603 test. This notebook also writes test_split.npz so notebook 05 can index everything against one shared, explicit definition of the test set.

Part 1 — Severity, with a Gamma GLM

2.3

Gamma distribution with a log link

Theory

Three properties of claim cost point at Gamma. It is strictly positive. It is right-skewed. And its errors are proportional — being €500 wrong on a €1,000 claim is a far worse miss than being €500 wrong on a €10,000 claim, and Gamma's variance grows with the mean, so it penalises errors that way automatically. The log link then guarantees positive predictions and makes the model multiplicative: each feature scales the expected claim by a factor rather than adding a fixed number of euros. Multiplicative rating factors are exactly the form actuaries and regulators already work in.

Evidence

Test MAE €1,102.26, RMSE €2,261.57, Gamma deviance 1.0595. Pseudo-R² is 0.0069 — essentially no variance explained, precisely as notebook 01 predicted.

2.4

Read the coefficients as multiplicative rating factors

Theory

Under a log link, exponentiating a coefficient converts it into a multiplier. A coefficient of 0.10 means "multiply the expected claim by e0.10 = 1.11", i.e. an 11% loading. This is the single biggest practical advantage of the GLM: the model is its rate table.

Evidence

Baseline claim (the intercept) is €1,162. Vehicle age is significant and negative (−0.0085, p<0.001) — older cars are worth less to repair. Bonus-malus is significant and positive but tiny (+0.0030). Several regions load claims by 24–34% (R94 ×1.34, R41 ×1.27, R93 ×1.25), which is plausible as regional labour and parts costs.

2.5

Fit an OLS regression alongside it, as a control

Theory

Claiming Gamma is the right choice is more convincing if you show what the naive alternative does. Ordinary least squares assumes symmetric, constant-variance errors and has no positivity constraint, so in principle it can predict a negative claim amount — an obviously meaningless answer.

Evidence

On this data the demonstration doesn't land: OLS produced 0 negative predictions and near-identical error (MAE €1,102.44 vs the GLM's €1,102.26). The cell prints a conclusion claiming OLS was worse, but that text is hardcoded and contradicts the output above it. The honest reading: because the features barely predict severity at all, both models collapse toward the same near-constant prediction, and the distributional choice has almost nothing to bite on. Gamma remains the right principled choice — the case for it is theoretical here, not empirical.

Part 2 — Frequency, with a Poisson GLM

2.6

Poisson distribution with a log link

Theory

The target is a count of claims — a non-negative integer — which is what Poisson is for. The log link again keeps predictions positive and makes risk factors multiplicative. Decision 1.5 already verified the variance-equals-mean assumption holds here.

Evidence

Test MAE 0.0988, RMSE 0.2370, Poisson deviance 43,585.73. Unlike severity, the coefficients are strongly significant: bonus-malus at z = 66.0, vehicle age at z = −29.8, driver age at z = 15.5.

2.7

Include log(Exposure) as an offset — supplied at fit time

Theory

Policies run for different fractions of a year. A policy active for six months has half the opportunity to claim, so comparing raw counts across policies is unfair. An offset is a predictor whose coefficient is forced to exactly 1; with a log link, adding log(Exposure) as an offset converts the model from one of claim counts into one of claim rates per year. That is what makes the coefficients mean "risk per unit time" rather than "risk per policy record".

Evidence

The offset must be passed when fitting, not only when predicting. An earlier version of this notebook supplied it only at predict time, which left the coefficients estimated on the wrong scale and made every frequency comparison in notebook 05 invalid. Bonus-malus now reads +0.0226 per point, i.e. each point multiplies the expected annual claim rate by 1.023.

2.8

Diagnose with calibration plots by risk band, not just headline error

Theory

MAE tells you the average size of an error but not where the model goes wrong. Grouping policies into bands and plotting actual against predicted rate within each band exposes systematic bias — the kind that makes a pricing model unfair to a specific customer segment even when its overall error looks fine.

Evidence

Two clear failures. Across bonus-malus bands the GLM underpredicts everywhere, by roughly 50–60% in the three lowest-risk bands. Across driver age it fits a gentle upward slope, completely missing the U-shape — the sharp spike at 18–25 and the uptick at 65+. Both are the direct consequence of forcing a straight-line effect: driver age enters as one linear term, so the model literally cannot bend.

Why this is the interesting result

The GLM's failures are structural, not accidental. It compresses predictions toward the mean and cannot represent a non-monotonic relationship. That is the precise gap notebooks 03 and 04 exist to test — and it sets up a clean hypothesis: if flexible models help anywhere, they should help on frequency and driver age specifically.

03

XGBoost — testing whether flexibility pays

notebooks/03_xgboost.ipynb → models/xgb_*.json, results/predictions_xgb.npz

Gradient boosted trees build many shallow decision trees in sequence, each one correcting the errors of the ensemble so far. Because trees split on thresholds, they find curves, cut-points and interactions without being told to — which is exactly what the GLM could not do.

3.1

Train severity on log(ClaimAmount), then exponentiate back

Theory

XGBoost's default squared-error loss treats a €500 miss identically whether the claim was €1,000 or €10,000, and the heavy right tail would let a few huge claims dominate training. Taking logs makes the target roughly symmetric — notebook 01 showed the log distribution is close to normal — so squared error on the log scale approximates proportional error on the euro scale, which is what Gamma achieves by design.

Evidence

MAE €938.56, a genuine 15% improvement on the GLM. But RMSE rises to €2,340.31 and Gamma deviance rises to 1.3611 from the GLM's 1.0595. That isn't a contradiction — it is the loss function showing through. Log-scale squared error and Gamma deviance are different objectives, so each model wins on the metric closest to what it optimised.

3.2

Handle exposure by modelling the rate and weighting by exposure

Theory

XGBoost has no native offset the way a GLM does, so the same adjustment has to be engineered. Setting the target to ClaimNb ÷ Exposure makes it a per-year rate, and setting each row's sample weight to its exposure restores the right influence — a policy observed for a full year counts more than one observed for a month. It reaches the same place as an offset by a different route.

Evidence

With objective='count:poisson', validation log-likelihood improves steadily from 1.4119 to 1.2526 over 500 rounds. Test Poisson deviance 41,478.64 — a 4.8% improvement on the GLM, and the best of the three models.

3.3

Constrain the trees: depth 4, 500 rounds, learning rate 0.05, 80% subsampling

Theory

Boosting overfits readily. Four levels of depth limits each tree to fairly simple interactions; a low learning rate makes each tree contribute a small correction so the ensemble converges gradually rather than lurching; and sampling 80% of rows and 80% of columns per tree decorrelates the trees, which is the same variance-reduction idea behind random forests.

Evidence

The severity model stops early — validation RMSE bottoms out around round 50 at 1.0983 and drifts up after — confirming there is little signal to extract. The frequency model was still improving at round 500, so it may be slightly under-trained.

Methodological caveat worth knowing

Early stopping here uses the test set as its evaluation set. That leaks a small amount of test information into the choice of how many trees to keep, so XGBoost's reported numbers are mildly optimistic relative to a fully clean holdout. The fix is a three-way train/validation/test split. The effect is small at this depth, but it should be stated rather than assumed away.

3.4

Report permutation importance alongside built-in gain importance

Theory

XGBoost's default importance measures total loss reduction from splits on a feature, which systematically favours features that get split on often — continuous variables, and high-frequency one-hot categories. Permutation importance instead shuffles one feature and measures how much performance degrades, which answers the question you actually care about: how much does the model rely on this?

Evidence

Gain importance puts VehBrand_B12 on top of the severity model by a wide margin, which mostly reflects it being a very common brand. The notebook flags this and adds a permutation run (10 repeats, scored on MAE) as the less biased view. On frequency, bonus-malus dominates on both measures — which is what a variable engineered to summarise claims history should do.

3.5

Use partial dependence plots to make the learned curve visible

Theory

A tree ensemble has no coefficient to quote, so its relationships must be recovered by probing it: vary one feature across its range, hold the others at their observed values, and plot the average prediction. This is the tooling that makes a tree model auditable instead of opaque, and it is what a regulator would expect to see in place of a coefficient table.

Evidence

This is the clearest win in the project. The driver-age curve shows the real U-shape — a sharp spike at 18–22, a minimum around 25–30, and a rise again for older drivers — which the GLM's single linear term erased entirely. The bonus-malus curve rises steeply with no plateau. Calibration by band improves to match: XGBoost tracks actual rates closely across all five bonus-malus bands including the 150+ tail, where the GLM badly underpredicted.

3.6

Judge the frequency model on lift and spread, not on MAE

Theory

For pricing, what matters is rank ordering — does the model put the riskier policy above the safer one? A model that predicts the base rate for everyone has excellent MAE and zero commercial value, because MAE rewards compressing predictions toward the mean. A lift chart sorts policies into deciles by predicted risk and checks whether actual claim rates climb across them.

Evidence

Actual claim rate rises monotonically from 0.0264 in the safest decile to 0.1288 in the riskiest — roughly 4.9×. Predicted rates span 0.017 to 1.388, with a 90th-to-10th-percentile spread of 3.6×. The top decile is over-predicted, though: an artefact of the rate target, since a short policy with one claim implies an inflated annualised rate the model partly learns.

04

Neural network — the flexibility ceiling

notebooks/04_neural_net.ipynb → models/nn_*.pt, models/nn_*_scaler.pkl, results/predictions_nn.npz

A feedforward network is the most flexible of the three and the least interpretable. Including it tests whether there is signal in this data that trees are leaving behind — and, if not, settles the question of whether the ceiling is the model or the data.

4.1

Standardise every feature before training

Theory

Gradient descent is sensitive to feature scale in a way that trees are not. Population density runs into the tens of thousands while driver age tops out at 99; without standardising, the loss surface is stretched along one axis and a single learning rate cannot suit both. Rescaling to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 makes the gradients comparable across features.

Evidence

The fitted scaler is saved next to each network. This matters more than it looks: a network without its scaler is unusable, because scoring new data requires the exact training mean and variance. The frequency and severity networks get separate scalers, each fitted on its own training distribution.

4.2

Keep the architecture small: two hidden layers, 64 and 32 units, dropout 0.2

Theory

Capacity should match the signal available. With 42 input columns and — as notebook 01 established — very weak severity signal, a deeper network would have more parameters to memorise noise with and nothing extra to learn. Dropout randomly zeroes 20% of activations during training, forcing the network not to depend on any single pathway.

Evidence

Severity: MAE €957.42, RMSE €2,349.08, Gamma deviance 1.4114. Statistically indistinguishable from XGBoost's €938.56 — a 2% gap. Three quite different model families landing in the same place is strong evidence that the limit is the data.

4.3

Read the training curve for underfitting, not just overfitting

Theory

The usual worry is validation loss turning upward while training loss keeps falling. The opposite pattern — both curves flattening at a similar level early — means the model has extracted everything the features contain and is limited by information, not capacity.

Evidence

Severity validation loss moves from 1.2710 to 1.2059 over 100 epochs and plateaus after roughly 60. The frequency network is more striking: validation loss goes 0.30249 → 0.30190 across the entire run — essentially flat from epoch 10, meaning it learned little beyond the base rate. Note the training loss prints higher than validation loss; that is dropout being active during training and switched off at evaluation, not a bug.

4.4

Reproduce the exposure adjustment as a custom weighted loss

Theory

The same exposure problem, solved a third way. The network predicts a rate, and the loss multiplies each sample's squared error by its exposure, so long policies dominate the gradient. Consistency across all three models here is what keeps the comparison fair.

Evidence

Test Poisson deviance 42,250.46 — better than the GLM's 43,585.73, worse than XGBoost's 41,478.64.

4.5

Clip negative predicted rates to zero, and flag it as a design flaw

Theory

The output layer is a plain linear unit, so nothing stops it producing a negative claim rate — a meaningless quantity. This is precisely the constraint a GLM's log link enforces structurally and a network does not. Clipping patches the symptom; a softplus or exponential output activation would fix the cause.

Evidence

Predicted rates run 0.0000 to 4.5948, with enough values pinned at exactly zero that decile boundaries collapse and the lift chart needs duplicates='drop' to build honest groups. The 90th-to-10th-percentile spread is only 2.4×, against XGBoost's 3.6× — measurably weaker risk discrimination.

05

Comparison and pure premium

notebooks/05_comparison.ipynb → results/18–23_*.png

The final notebook combines the two stages into a price and decides which model to recommend. Its most important design decisions are about discipline rather than statistics.

5.1

Fit nothing here — load everything

Theory

If a comparison notebook refits the models it is comparing, it produces a different fit from the one each modelling notebook reported, and the two sets of numbers drift apart with nobody noticing. Loading saved predictions makes the comparison provably about the same models the earlier notebooks documented.

Evidence

The notebook imports only numpy, pandas, matplotlib and json — no torch, xgboost, statsmodels or sklearn. An earlier version refitted all six models here, duplicating every fit and stalling the kernel. Each modelling notebook now writes predictions_*.npz and metrics_*.json, and this one asserts the array shapes match before comparing anything.

5.2

Score the severity model on the frequency test rows, inside the modelling notebook

Theory

Pure premium is frequency × severity for the same policy. The frequency and severity models are trained on different tables with different row counts, so the severity model has to be re-scored on the frequency test policies to produce a matched pair. Doing that where the row identity is unambiguous — inside the notebook that owns the split — is what keeps the two halves aligned.

Evidence

An earlier version built the severity half by taking a positional tail slice of the unshuffled matrix while the frequency half came from the shuffled split. The two row sets overlapped by 0.1997 — pure chance. Every pure premium figure and every band table derived from it was wrong. This is the most instructive bug in the project: it produced plausible-looking numbers and no error message.

5.3

Lead the comparison with Gini, not with MAE

Theory

The Gini coefficient measures how well a model rank-orders risk: sort policies by prediction, then ask how concentrated actual claims are among the top-ranked. It is normalised so 1 is a perfect ranker and 0 is random. This is the right lead metric because a rating structure only has to get relativities right — a model can be mis-calibrated at every level and still price fairly, whereas a model with good MAE and no discrimination cannot price at all.

Evidence

Computed from the saved GLM artifacts for this document: frequency Gini 0.266, pure premium Gini 0.137. The drop between them is the severity stage diluting a decent frequency ranking — a direct consequence of severity being near-unpredictable.

5.4

Anchor pure premium against an empirical benchmark

Theory

A modelled premium needs a sanity check that doesn't come from a model. Portfolio claim rate × mean claim size gives the average cost per policy-year the test portfolio actually incurred. A model landing an order of magnitude away from that is signalling a bug, not a finding — exactly the check that would have caught the misalignment in 5.2.

Evidence

Benchmark: 0.1011 claims/year × €1,558 = €157.52. The GLM's mean pure premium is €173.75 (median €147.83) — the right order of magnitude, and slightly above the benchmark as a mean pulled by a right tail should be.

5.5

Treat interpretability as a judgement, and say so

Theory

Under APRA's Prudential Standard GPS 116, rating factors must be documented and defensible. That is a real constraint with no natural numeric scale, so the notebook hardcodes High/Medium/Low ratings and labels them explicitly as the only non-computed values on the page. Dressing a judgement up as a measurement would be worse than stating it as a judgement.

Evidence

GLM — high interpretability, low regulatory risk: coefficients exponentiate straight into the multiplicative rating factors regulators already read. XGBoost — medium: defensible with SHAP, partial dependence and monotonicity constraints, but not self-documenting. Neural net — low: materially more explanation work with no structural guarantees, so it must clear a higher accuracy bar to be worth the cost.

Reading the table

Frequency has a clear winner. XGBoost leads on all three metrics, improving Poisson deviance by 4.8% over the GLM. Small in absolute terms, but it is the actuarially correct metric and the direction is unambiguous — and the partial dependence plots show exactly where the gain comes from, in the driver-age curve the GLM could not bend around.

Severity has no winner, and that is the finding. XGBoost takes MAE by 15%, the GLM takes Gamma deviance by 28%, and each wins on the metric nearest its own loss function. Three model families spanning a huge range of flexibility land within a few percent of each other. The ceiling is in the features, not the algorithms.

MAE and RMSE disagreeing is informative, not noisy. RMSE squares errors, so it punishes the occasional large miss; MAE does not. XGBoost winning MAE while losing RMSE means it is closer on typical claims and further out on extreme ones — which is what you would expect from a model that is allowed to spread its predictions instead of hugging the mean.

What the project actually shows

five conclusions a student should leave with
i

Know which half of your problem is predictable

Why

Frequency is learnable from these features; severity is very nearly not. Splitting the problem made that visible. A single model of total cost would have averaged a strong signal with a non-existent one and left you unable to tell which was which — and unable to know where extra modelling effort would pay.

ii

Model complexity buys ranking power, not accuracy

Why

The headline error metrics barely move between a GLM and a neural network. What moves is the shape of what the model can represent: the driver-age U-curve, calibration in the worst bonus-malus band, a 3.6× spread of predicted rates instead of a compressed one. For pricing, relativities are the product — so measure discrimination, not average error.

iii

Match the metric to the loss, and read disagreements carefully

Why

The GLM wins Gamma deviance because it optimises Gamma deviance. XGBoost wins MAE because it optimises squared error on a log scale, which is closer to absolute error in euros. Neither result is evidence that one model "understands" the data better. When metrics disagree, the first question is always which objective each model was actually minimising.

iv

Data bugs impersonate model limitations

Why

Overdispersion of 1.33 looked like grounds for switching to a Negative Binomial model. It was nine bad records; after capping, the ratio fell to 1.065 and Poisson was fine. The misaligned pure premium rows produced numbers that looked entirely reasonable and were meaningless. Neither announced itself. Sanity anchors — an empirical benchmark, a shape assertion, an overlap check — are what turn silent wrongness into a visible failure.

v

In a regulated market, explainability is a constraint, not a preference

Why

A 4.8% deviance gain has to be weighed against the documentation burden of defending a tree ensemble under GPS 116. The defensible reading of these results is a split decision: XGBoost for frequency, where the gain is real and traceable to a specific learned curve, and the GLM for severity, where nothing outperforms it on its own metric and it costs nothing to explain. The reasonable alternative — GLM throughout, with XGBoost used as a challenger model to find the non-linearities and then encoding them as GLM terms — is standard practice, and worth considering before reaching for the more complex production model.
